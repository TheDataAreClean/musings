> _Today is the day India achieved independence from British rule. What stories have you grown up with around this day?_
> 
> – Prompt from [IndieWebClub Bangalore](https://blr.indiewebclub.org/)[ ](https://blr.indiewebclub.org/)meetup #32.

***

I have never truly felt connected to the idea and celebration of Independence Day in India. Probably because I have only interacted with stories about it. I am aware that it is a good thing to be independent of British rule, but the idea has always been an abstraction in my head. These days it has also become hard to feel independent in today's India, which has taken me even farther from the concept.

Today is also the day [The Partition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_India) began. That is what I remember when I think of Independence Day. Growing up in my family, this was the part that felt more significant. My grandmother was 12 years old during the partition, and travelled through horrific circumstances, leaving behind a home that we now call Pakistan.

The 15th of August was always just a public holiday to me. Partition was the part that felt real – even though I never knew the before, only the after, and how it shaped my family.

***

My grandmother used to tell us her partition stories when my siblings and I were kids. Much later in my life, I realised how much trauma that must have left in a 12-year-old. She wanted to share it with all of us and make us a part of her life.

Unfortunately, I do not remember it all too well now; I was too young to soak it in properly. She tried to hand over her stories to us, and they slipped through anyway. We probably were not old enough to hold them the way they should've been held.

Growing older, I have come to better understand (or at least imagine) how that time shaped her, and as a result, my parents. The impact of it has reached me anyway, even if not in the way I would have preferred.

***

As she has grown older, I crave a lucid conversation with my grandmother now. I want to retrace her journey and walk through it with her – the home she left, the effort she made to get here, the parts of it she could not fully share with us when we were younger. I want to sit with her, and archive our family's story before it's gone.

I have been making some plans around this for nearly a year now. I want to record her stories as audio, add pictures from family albums where I can find them. But I am not actually doing it.

***

Going back and spending time with my grandmother also means engaging with my family about it, which has never been an easy proposition for me. It would mean involving my family in the process, and I find it hard to trust that they will understand why I want to do this.

My relationship with my family has not had the best last few years. Bringing up my grandmother's partition story, and asking to record it, means asking for a kind of openness we don't usually practice with each other. I don't know how they'll take that.

This hesitance and fear have existed for me nearly as long as the idea to archive my family's history. I keep putting it off, trading it for my comfort, over something I won't be able to get back later.

Maybe that's why I want to do this in the first place. Understanding partition and her life after it, might tell me where the distance between me and my family came from, why trust doesn't come easy.

The story of a 12-year-old girl might be the same story as why we are the way we are. I have just never gone looking for it. Maybe some part of me does not want to find out.
