> _What are some digital rituals you participate in?_
> 
> – Prompt from [Bangalore IndieWebClub](https://blr.indiewebclub.org/) meetup #33.

***

I have loved links for as long as I can remember. Links to blogs, to videos, to images, to apps, to papers, to obscure ideas on the other side of the planet. Another thing I have loved nearly as much is ~~collecting~~ curating them and sharing them with friends and family, even though I know they do not check out most of them (sigh).

Sharing is one part of the appeal. More importantly, I love coming back to the links I have saved. Each one feels like an old entry in my diary, a recollection of something I cared about enough to save it. Over time, the collection becomes a record of my attention. A journal, or a trail of what I found worth keeping.

***

### A graveyard of failed attempts

In my love of links also lies a problem. It usually begins when a critical quantity of links have been saved, and now they need to be organised into a searchable archive. This is where things start going downhill.

I have tried dozens of “second brain” tools and paid obnoxious amounts in subscription fees over the years to satiate this obsession with links. Most of these tools make the same promises: put everything here, organise it well, and you will always be able to return to your ideas for perpetuity.

The tricky part being “_organise it well_”.

All of these tools demand that you maintain a structure, one they have decided is the best way to build your system. It usually involves some combination of folders, tags, databases, links, naming conventions, and rules about where things belong. Commit to the vocabulary of the application, and you will succeed!

You decide to take the leap and commit to a structure, one that mostly makes sense when you create it. But as you collect more links, explore newer corners and vast depths of the web, the way you categorise these links and your own ideas keeps evolving.

A category that felt obvious six months ago starts feeling arbitrary. Two interests that once seemed separate turn out to be connected. Some links matter before there is a language for why they matter, and then every characteristic about it is being manually labelled and categorised every time.

Eventually, maintaining the system becomes the task, and saving and exploring links loses its meaning.

Perhaps some amount of entropy is not a failure of the system. It is simply a reflection of how our interests grow and evolve.

***

### A place for almost anything

As I was walking the halls of despair, dejected by my failure to organise my beloved links, I came across [_mymind_](https://mymind.com/). Another app that promises to solve all the problems I have been complaining about.

“_Remember everything. Organize nothing._” read their tagline. What a concept!

I was cynical at first, but I am also addicted to the idea of completely uprooting and porting my digital presence every three months. So I gave it a shot.

Soon enough, I was saving everything I wanted to preserve and remember in the app: articles, images, notes, repositories, music, books, products, and things I did not even know could be saved. Finding them again was painless too. I could type a keyword, an idea, or even a visual characteristic, and the app would surface related links for me.

That was more than three years ago. Now, more than 1,800 items later, I cannot imagine my life without _mymind_.

At this point, I should probably disclose that this is not an advertisement for the app, even though I absolutely love it and could not be convinced otherwise.

***

### A place like my actual mind?

In _mymind_, my thoughts do not live in neat folders. They move more like objects flowing in a river. I notice one, pick it up, examine it, connect it to something else, and then drop it back into the current. I may return to it later in a different context.

_mymind_ feels closer to my actual mind than most second-brain tools ever have.

There is no rigid system asking me to decide what something is or where it belongs at the moment I save it. It is a forgiving system that lets me collect ideas freely and return to them when I please.

Probably my favourite feature of the app is **Serendipity**, a tab where the app resurfaces old links at random, presenting you with the opportunity to keep or forget each one.

There are so many features I have not even talked about here. I highly recommend checking out [_mymind_](https://mymind.com/) for yourself.

***

### A dream for software

My experience with _mymind_ also points to a larger belief I hold about software.

At the risk of borrowing from Marie Kondo, software should spark joy when you use it. Software should adapt to the way the user thinks and works. It should help you do something you care about without constantly reminding you, or more accurately, shouting from the rooftops, about its own infrastructure and systems.

Too much software forces people to adapt to arbitrary choices shaped by the priorities of the system. You must use its categories, follow its workflow, and maintain the structure it expects. The burden of compatibility falls on you.

Entropy is inevitable in any system that grows with a person. The challenge for software is not to eliminate it, but to absorb it without passing the burden back to the user.

The best software makes you forget that this infrastructure exists at all. It becomes invisible because it adapts to how you want to work. It can lead when you need direction, follow when you know where you are going, and move alongside you as your habits and interests evolve.

As _mymind_ becomes a more integral part of my life, I also realise that I have barely scratched the surface of what it can be for me. There are still so many ways to return to the things I have saved, find old ideas in new contexts, and discover connections I did not know were there. More than three years in, I am still learning how to use it, and look forward to the next turn in this journey.
