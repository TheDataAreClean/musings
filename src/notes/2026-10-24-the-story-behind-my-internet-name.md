# Part #1: Plurality

When I say the word “**data**”, what comes to your mind?

Do you think of one body of information or many individual pieces? This has been a point of low-to-mild contention in the data world for years.

I land on the plural side of the argument, and write sentences like “The data are ready for analysis” or “Many of these data were collected over years”. My thinking has evolved from data being the plural form of the Latin word datum, meaning “a single piece of information”. 

That is where the first part of my internet name comes from: _thedataare_.

There is also this strip from _[PhD Comics](https://phdcomics.com/comics.php?f=1816)_ about the same topic.

# Part #2: Insanity

“Does this job have analytics?”

“When will I do data science?”

“I want to work on models.”

In the early years of my career, I would hear these questions regularly, often weekly, from interns and young professionals working with me. Every time, I felt intense rage at hearing some version of them.

So many of these new joiners had gone through prohibitively expensive and extremely useless data science boot camps. They had been shown an ideal world where a magical stork delivers a dataset and they get straight to “building models” and “doing machine learning”.

{% marginnote "boot camps" %}
i wonder how many such boot camps still exist? are they teaching kids “ai” these days? probably. sigh.
{% endmarginnote %}

Somewhere between the expectations those boot camps had set and the pointless conversations we were having, the most fundamental questions kept getting left out. Where does the data come from? What happens once you have it? How do you use it to understand something?

I would keep asking them, do you even understand your data well enough to make these assumptions? To run these so-called “models”? Never got a convincing answer to my questions, and the interns would try to do what they want anyway. When the results made no sense, they seemed to think they were missing some secret sauce for analysing the data. It was rarely questioned how little they knew about the data itself.

I often point out the importance of getting to know the data, spending time with it, and cleaning it before attempting even the simplest analysis. That frustration is where the “clean” in _thedataareclean_ comes from.

Even today, understanding and cleaning the data remain the most important part of any analysis for me.
